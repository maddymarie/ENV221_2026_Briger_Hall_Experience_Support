The Natural Environment is the Built Environment: Natural Materials as Structural Features in Princeton’s Briger Hall

The Natural Environment is the Built Environment: Natural Materials as Structural Features in Princeton’s Briger Hall

Look up. The beams and columns holding up much of this building are not hidden behind plaster. They are wood, left exposed on purpose. Why build a science building out of trees, and is wood really better for the planet than steel and concrete?

1 Find it

Where to find it: Enter Briger Hall Entrance H3, Off of Ivy Lane

Look closely: Notice the structural support surrounding you and compare it to other buildings you've seen. Briger Hall uses exposed mass timber to provide structural support, compared to conventional steel or concrete.

Wood was not used everywhere. Princeton’s planners used mass timber in offices and shared spaces, and steel and concrete in laboratories, which need tighter control of vibration. [2] The designers say the exposed timber reduces the building’s embodied carbon (the emissions from making, transporting and assembling materials) and is meant to be a visible signal that sustainability is part of the building’s identity. [3] According to STRUCTURE magazine, carbon stored in the mass timber and wood cladding contributes to a 26% reduction in the building’s embodied carbon. [7]

The wood travelled a long way. The timber supplier, HASSLACHER, is based in Austria, and the construction team visited its plant there. [6]

3 What if / What might change?

Hypothetical alternative

Imagine Briger Hall’s offices and shared spaces built the same way as its labs: a steel frame with concrete floors, covered by drywall and ceiling tiles. You can see a version of this alternative in the same complex, in the lab areas.

For the environment: Most of a building material’s carbon footprint comes from making it, not moving it. In one U.S. study, the manufacturing stage made up about 94–96% of a mass timber building’s embodied emissions, and the mass timber designs had 22–50% less embodied carbon than matching concrete buildings. [4] Cement production alone causes at least 8% of human-caused CO₂ emissions, because making cement takes high heat and releases CO₂ from limestone. [5] A steel-and-concrete version would therefore probably have higher emissions upfront. Shipping wood from Austria adds emissions, but transport is usually a small share of the total. Wood also stores the carbon the tree absorbed for as long as the building stands. However, critics argue that this benefit is often overstated, because cutting trees reduces the carbon stored in forests for decades. [8]

For people: Because the wood is the finish, the building needs fewer extra layers of material, and many people find exposed wood warm and calming. It also teaches: anyone who looks up can see how the building stands. The tradeoffs are that wood has to be engineered to resist fire (here, a two-hour fire rating) and to block sound between floors [1], and it could not meet the vibration needs of the labs [2]. Steel and concrete are familiar, widely available materials, while mass timber needs specialist suppliers. That is one reason this wood came from overseas.

What we do not know: Whether mass timber is truly better for the climate depends on assumptions we could not check. How was the forest managed and replanted? How much carbon was lost in harvesting? What happens to the wood when the building is eventually torn down? If the wood ends up rotting or burning, much of the stored carbon returns to the air. We also do not know exactly how this building’s carbon figures were calculated.

5 Listen and look

Read the complete audio transcript

Stop for a moment and look up. Find a place where a wooden beam meets a wooden column. Look at the end of the beam. It gets narrower and slots straight into the column, so wood rests on wood and there is almost no metal showing. Now look at the side of the beam. Those thin stripes are layers of wood glued together. This is called glulam, a kind of mass timber, and it holds up much of Briger Hall.

Why build a science building out of wood? Mostly to reduce embodied carbon, the emissions from making, shipping and assembling a building’s materials. Steel and especially concrete are carbon-heavy. Cement production alone causes at least eight percent of human carbon dioxide emissions. Wood also stores the carbon the tree absorbed while it grew. The designers left the timber exposed so it works as the finish and as a visible sign of the building’s environmental goals.

Now imagine a different version of this building. This is hypothetical. Picture these offices and shared spaces built like the labs nearby: a steel frame, concrete floors, and everything hidden behind drywall and ceiling tiles. The labs really are built this way, because sensitive experiments need floors that barely vibrate, and wood could not provide that.

For the environment, the steel-and-concrete version would probably mean more emissions before anyone even walked in. Studies of comparable buildings have found mass timber cutting embodied carbon by roughly a fifth to a half. The wood here was supplied by a company in Austria, so shipping adds some emissions, but research suggests manufacturing, not transport, is where most of a material’s footprint comes from.

For people, the tradeoffs go both ways. Wood gives a warmer space that you can learn from just by looking up. But it has to be engineered to resist fire and block sound, it relies on specialist suppliers, and it cannot do every job, as the labs show.

And here is an honest uncertainty. Not everyone agrees mass timber helps the climate. Some researchers, including one here at Princeton, argue that cutting trees releases forest carbon that takes decades to recover. The answer depends on how the forest was managed and what happens to this wood when the building comes down one day.

So, before you move on, look at the beam above you and ask yourself: if this building lasts a hundred years, is that long enough for the tree it came from to be replaced? And who should be responsible for making sure it is?
